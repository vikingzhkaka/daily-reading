How Plants Warn Each Other

Most people picture a forest as a collection of silent individuals, each tree competing for light and water. Recent research suggests a rather different picture. Plants, it turns out, exchange information constantly, and they do so through channels that we were unable to detect until quite recently.

The clearest example involves damage. When a caterpillar begins to chew on a tomato leaf, the wounded plant releases a mixture of chemicals into the air. Neighbouring tomato plants absorb these signals and, within hours, begin producing bitter compounds that make their own leaves less attractive to insects. The warning is not deliberate in any human sense, yet the effect is real: those neighbours suffer noticeably less damage than plants kept in isolation.

Underground, the system is even more remarkable. The roots of most trees are wrapped in fine threads of fungus, which extend through the soil and link one root system to another. Because these threads can carry sugars, water and chemical messages, biologists sometimes describe the network as a kind of forest internet. Experiments in which a single tree was shaded have shown that carbon may flow through the fungus to its struggling neighbour, although researchers still argue about who really benefits from the transfer.

Why should a plant help a rival? One explanation is that many of the trees joined by such networks are relatives, so assisting a neighbour indirectly protects shared genes. Another is that the fungus, which depends on healthy hosts for its own food, may be managing the flow for its own advantage. Whichever account proves correct, the familiar image of the forest as a battlefield is clearly incomplete.

The practical value of this research is considerable. If crops can be persuaded to warn one another, farmers may eventually need fewer chemical sprays; and if soil fungi matter as much as the evidence now suggests, then heavy ploughing must be reconsidered. A forest, in other words, may be less a crowd of strangers than a community that has been quietly talking for millions of years.

中文译文

大多数人把森林想象成一群沉默的个体，每棵树都在争夺光和水。近来的研究给出的却是一幅相当不同的图景。原来植物一直在不断交换信息，而它们所用的通道，我们直到不久前还无法察觉。

最清楚的例子与受损有关。当一条毛虫开始啃咬番茄叶片时，受伤的植株会向空气中释放一种混合化学物质。邻近的番茄植株吸收这些信号后，在几小时内就开始产生苦味化合物，使自己的叶子对昆虫不那么有吸引力。这种预警在任何人类意义上都不是有意为之，但效果是真实的：那些邻居受到的损害明显少于被隔离种植的植株。

在地下，这套系统更加了不起。多数树木的根都被真菌的细丝包裹着，这些细丝穿过土壤延伸，把一套根系与另一套连接起来。由于这些细丝能够运送糖分、水和化学信息，生物学家有时把这个网络描述为一种森林互联网。有些实验把单独一棵树遮上光，结果显示碳可能通过真菌流向那个陷入困境的邻居，尽管研究者仍在争论这种转移究竟让谁真正受益。

植物为什么要帮助竞争对手呢？一种解释是，被这类网络连接起来的许多树木彼此是亲缘关系，所以帮助邻居就间接保护了共有的基因。另一种解释是，真菌本身要靠健康的宿主获取食物，它也许是在为自己的好处调控这种流动。无论哪种说法最终正确，把森林视为战场的那个熟悉形象显然是不完整的。

这项研究的实际价值相当可观。如果能让作物互相预警，农民最终或许可以少用化学喷剂；而如果土壤真菌的重要性真如现有证据所显示的那样，那么深耕就必须重新考虑。换句话说，森林与其说是一群陌生人，不如说是一个已经安静地交谈了数百万年的共同体。
