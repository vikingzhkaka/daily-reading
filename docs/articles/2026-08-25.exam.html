Why Museums Are Rewriting Their Labels

For more than a century, the museum label was written for the specialist. A small card beside a bronze vessel would list its dynasty, its excavation site and its catalogue number, and nothing else. Curators assumed that visitors either possessed the background knowledge to interpret such data or would acquire it elsewhere. That assumption is now being abandoned across the profession, and the reason has little to do with fashion.

Surveys conducted in Britain and the Netherlands found that the average visitor spends fewer than fifteen seconds in front of an individual exhibit. Faced with a label that offered dates rather than meaning, most people simply walked on. Attendance figures remained healthy, yet learning outcomes did not improve, which suggested that the public was consuming museums as scenery instead of using them as classrooms. Since the majority of national collections depend on public funding, this gap between visiting and understanding gradually became a political problem as well as an educational one.

The response has been a quiet revolution in wording. Instead of opening with a date, many labels now open with a question or a human detail: who made the object, what it cost its maker, why anyone bothered. A Roman lamp is introduced not as a second-century artefact but as an item that once let a child read after sunset. Because such framing invites the visitor to imagine a situation rather than memorise a fact, it tends to hold attention long enough for the historical information to be absorbed afterwards.

Critics warn that the trend may go too far. If every exhibit must be made instantly relatable, they argue, curators risk flattening the strangeness of the past into something comfortably modern. There is also the danger of oversimplification, whereby genuine scholarly uncertainty disappears behind a confident sentence written for children. Museums that have handled the shift well appear to share one habit: they layer their text, offering a short accessible line first and fuller academic detail below, so that neither audience is sacrificed for the other.

What this debate ultimately reveals is a change in how cultural institutions define their duty. A museum that merely preserves objects has done half its job; one that helps ordinary people make sense of them has done the whole of it.

中文译文

一个多世纪以来，博物馆的展签是为专家写的。一件青铜器旁的小卡片会列出它的朝代、出土地点和藏品编号，别无其他。策展人假定参观者要么具备解读这些数据的背景知识，要么会在别处获得这些知识。如今整个行业都在放弃这一假定，而原因与潮流无关。

在英国和荷兰进行的调查发现，普通参观者在单件展品前停留不足十五秒。面对一张只提供日期而非意义的展签，大多数人干脆走开了。参观人数依然可观，但学习效果并未改善——这说明公众把博物馆当成布景在消费，而不是当作课堂在使用。由于多数国家级藏品依赖公共资金，这种"参观"与"理解"之间的落差逐渐既成了教育问题，也成了政治问题。

回应是一场用词上的静默革命。许多展签现在不再以日期开头，而是以一个问题或一处人性细节开头：谁做的、做它花了制作者什么代价、为什么有人肯费这个工夫。一盏罗马油灯的介绍不再是"二世纪器物"，而是"一件曾让孩子在日落后还能读书的物品"。因为这种表述邀请参观者去想象一个情境而非记住一个事实，它往往能把注意力留住足够久，使历史信息随后得以被吸收。

批评者警告说，这一趋势可能走得太远。他们认为，如果每件展品都必须立刻变得可亲近，策展人就有把过去的陌生感压平成某种舒适的现代感的风险。还有过度简化的危险：真实的学术不确定性会消失在一句为孩子写的自信句子背后。那些较好地处理了这一转变的博物馆似乎都有一个共同习惯：他们把文字分层，先给一句简短易懂的话，下面再给更完整的学术细节，这样任何一方观众都不必为另一方被牺牲。

这场争论最终揭示的，是文化机构对自身职责定义的改变。一座仅仅保存文物的博物馆只做完了一半工作；一座帮助普通人读懂它们的博物馆，才算做完了全部。
